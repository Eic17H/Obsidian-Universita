# Point location

An activity that you maybe you do every day. What does point location raise in your mind? For example, you're given geographical coordinates and need to find them on a map. It's something that we do so naturally we may forget it's not so simple.

When we say *point* we know it's an entity that can exist in any dimension, but in our case we're talking about a 2D point. If you think about a 2D point on the surface of Earth, of course it's actually a 3D point, but we can approximate it as a 2D point because the Earth is flat.

When you use satellites to locate yourself, they give you your UTS coordinates. Unless you're in the ocean and navigating from Europe to America, what can you do yourself with your UTF coordinates? Nothing, you just use them to locate yourself on a map.

But you need to know *what* a map is. Do you know how to define a map? Is it just a collection of points? What is a map? What do we have on a map? We have streets on maps, those are edges. But we also have Sardinia on a map, and what is it? A polytope.

So a digital map has:
* Points ($0\text D$);
* Edges, collection of ($1\text D$);
* Faces ($2\text D$).

How can we store that? Of course, a [[DCEL]]. The geometry of my map is given by just the position of the vertices. So the coordinates of the vertices is the geometrical (or geographical if you will) information. And we have our query point $q=(q_x,q_y)$. The question is "where is $q$ in the map"? There are three possibilities: on a face, on an edge or on a vertex, where the latter two are special cases. Let's just focus on "where" meaning "which face".

Let's imagine $q$ is inside a face, define an algorithm to know which face. How do we know whether a point is inside a polygon? Not simple at all if the polygon is concave. We can [[polygon triangulation|triangulate]] it and now it's just convex faces. We call this step the *refinement of the ==subdivision== $S$*.

Once we have the triangles, what can we do? Check each triangle. We store for each triangle the ID of which face it's part of, then once we find the correct triangle we know which face it's in, and because a triangulation is a partition, a triangle belongs entirely to a single face. We find out if a point is inside a triangle using matrices.

Now let's compute the computational complexity. For that, we need to remember the Euler formula: $v-e+f=2$.

* Triangulation: $O(n \log n)$ ==🟠once==
* Search: $O(n)$ ==🔴every time==

How many times do we need to perform a search? Too many times. We aren't following a useful strategy:

> **Good strategy!**
> Look for the solution where the solution is.
> *Grazie al cazzo*

Of course, that means that we should *arrange our data* to make our work easier. Let's go back to our ==subdivision== $S$.

The problem is that triangles are unsorted. What can be a different way to split our faces so that the parts are sortable and convex? A different refining of $S$. A refining is a partition into convex and easily sortable parts. We want to sort something, it's better to work with just one coordinate.

I refine my subdivision with a set of vertical lines. And actually, the set of vertical lines passing through each vertex.

![[Pasted image 20261118094435.png]]

(Pretend some of them are vertically aligned).

The problem now is that the parts aren't all convex... right? Some of them are triangles, the others are quadrilaterals, with at least one pair of parallel sides. That shape is called a *trapezoid* (*trapezio*).

Can a trapezoid be convex? Let's prove it can't: draw two parallel lines, can an angle with another side be concave? No. So all trapezoids are convex. Triangles are degenerate trapezoids (one side has length $0$.)

And in some ways, trapezoids are even nicer than triangles. What do we want to achieve? We want to achieve the partition into convex polygons that we can sort.

Now we can think of a strategy to accelerate the search. Look at one single portion of the plane in between two consecutive vertical lines. First of all, this portion has a name, it's called a *==slab==*. We have at most $2n-1$ slabs (because there can be disjoint faces, in our case it's much less), so it's $O(n)$.

What's inside a slab? Can there be a vertex inside a slab? No, if there was one, there would be a vertical line passing through it, $\tfrac{a \vdash \bot}{\lnot a}$.

> Inside a slab there are portions of edges completely crossing it.
> (As in, portions of edges such that those edges are edges that start and end not strictly inside the slab).

Let's reformulate:
1. In which slab is $q$? 1D search: build a BST with the $x$'s of the vertices of $S$. $O(\log n)$.
2. Portions of edges completely crossing it: I can sort the edges in $y$, meaning I can sort the portions in $y$. $\forall$ slab, build a BST with the $y$'s of the parts of the edges crossing it.
3. Find in which face is the portion? $O(\log n)$.

So, finding the slab is $O(\log n)$, going from a slab to a face is $O(\log n)$, the total cost is the sum, $O(\log n)$. Wow. We went from a simple and effective solution in $O(n)$ to a search in a sorted set.

> So, we're done, we have solved the problem, we can go home, we can take a spritz. A pear juice would be better at this time.

We didn't mention the cost of building the BST, $O(n \log n)$, but that's preprocessing, that's updating Google Maps (once in a while) while we care about the cost of querying (million times a second).

### Problem

Do you see any problem?

We ignored the point being on an edge or a vertex but it's not a problem.

No, the problem is related to a kind of computational complexity we never care about: memory complexity. $O(n)$ for the BST on $x$, we have $O(n)$ slabs and make a BST for each of them, $O(n)$ again, making it... $O(n^2)$.

> Can you see it? If you have a billion points, one billion of billions is a *==🟢pentallion==*, I don't even know what to call it.

Look at this vertex. It's inducing a split on these faces, but there's no relation between that vertex and those faces.

![[Pasted image 20261118101254.png]]

If we split faces using only vertical lines induced by the vertices of ==THE face==, then it already works. If I add vertical lines from other edges, it's useless, I'm just splitting trapezoids into other trapezoids. No need to draw vertical lines through the whole plane. Instead, if I limit myself to the lines induced by the vertices of the face, I'm making the least possible splits. *==Vertical extensions of the vertices==*.

Let's redefine how I can define my subdivision.

This time, for reasons that will be clear next week, the vertices are in ==general position== (no two vertices share the same $x$). Also, I'm putting my subdivision inside a bounding box. What does it mean that I'm using only vertical extensions of the vertices? It means that I go up and down from each vertex and stop as soon as I hit something (an edge).
