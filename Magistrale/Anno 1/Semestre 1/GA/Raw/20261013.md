# Polygon triangulation

The convex hull itself is a polygon, and it's a special polygon. Now we'll see other types of polygons.

Polygon triangulation can be thought of as an algorithm of the class ==*divide and conquer*==: you take a problem, you split your problem in smaller parts that are easier to be solved, then, once you have the easiest possible solution to your problem, then you collect the solutions to all your small problems into one big solution. Merge sort is one example.

Now, *why* do we care about polygon triangulation? Let's ease into it by looking at another problem, *art gallery guarding*:

> Imagine that you are asked by an art gallery (a museum). Imagine something completely hypothetical, imagine that in a big museum such as Louvre, completely hypothetically, as the Louvre is a big museum that's perfectly guarded, imagine it's vulnerable to jewelry theft. Imagine that you, as a computer scientist, are tasked to design a control system capable of keeping in sight the whole museum with the ==least== number of cameras.
> The cameras are pivoting, as in, they can rotate.
> The museum is approximated as a 2D environment, by imagining all the walls are vertical.
> The rooms are small enough to have the desired detail from any distance within the room.

Nice problem. How much do you want to be paid for this? Anyway, **==*least*==**, that's the most important part. If you have a simple square room, you can put the single camera anywhere and it will work. The problem is from 1973, before the term *computational geometry* was introduced as Shamos (?)'s PhD thesis title, and it became a field of CS. Though even before we knew we could solve geometrical problems with CS, as with this problem.

As long as the room is convex, we only need one camera. This follows from the definition of *convex*. When we say *in sight* we mean *straight lines*, also known as *segments*. So, we need to find the minimum amount of cameras such that, for all points in the room, we can trace a segment between that point and at least one camera such that it doesn't cross the boundaries.

> **Every triangle is convex**

Why can't there be a concave triangle? Because the sum of the angles of a triangle is $180°$, but a concave polygon needs at least one angle $>180°$, so the simplest concave polygon is a quadrilateral.

![[Pasted image 20261013113310.png]]

That means that if we divide a polygon into triangles, we can split it into convex portions. So that's our problem:

1. Split (partition) the polygon into ==convex== portions;
2. Control each portion with a camera.

When partitioning, the intersection between two partitions is always empty, there is no overlap.

> **Partition of $S$**
> $P_S=\{S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_n\}$ is a set of $n$ sets so that:
> 1. $\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^n S_i = S$;
> 2. $\forall i,j\ \ S_i \cap S_j = \emptyset$.

Let's partition a polygon. Let's pick a point inside it and create segments from it to the boundary of the polygon.

All partitions are convex in this case, but it could've been a coincidence, the procedure was random.

Let's do the same, but only using diagonals:

> **Diagonal**: segment connecting two vertices of a polygon, such that all points or the segment are inside the polygon, and the segment is not an edge of the polygon.

In a convex polygon, all segments connecting two vertices other than edges are diagonals.

By using diagonals, I don't introduce any new vertices.

![[Pasted image 20261013114400.png]]

This concave shape only has one diagonal. A segment connecting the bottom two vertices would be outside the polygon. So there's only one way to partition this polygon with diagonals.

By doing this, I split up the polygon into two convex polygons. I **triangulated** it.

In triangulation, I keep drawing diagonals that don't cross existing diagonals until I can't anymore.

![[Pasted image 20261013115157.png]]

We can't add any more diagonals. But we could've done it another way. The number of possible triangulations is the Catalan number of the amount of vertices, which is in $O(n!)$.

This polygon has $13$ vertices, this triangulation has $11$ triangles and $10$ diagonals.

Still $11$ triangles and $10$ diagonals. Of course, I swapped a diagonal for another, and each quadrilateral can be split into two triangles only so I swapped two triangles for two different triangles.

Let's look at the concave shape from earlier, $4$ vertices, $2$ triangles and $1$ diagonal.

> Conjecture: if a polygon has $n$ vertices, its triangulation has $n-2$ triangles and $n-3$ diagonals.

Let's look at a hexagon.

![[Pasted image 20261013115630.png]]

Well, for each vertex, I can form a diagonal with all the vertices that are neither the same ($1$) or adjacent ($2$), that being, $n-3$. I can form triangles with two diagonals and an edge, or two edges and a diagonal when one of the edges is adjacent to the vertex I'm considering (the ajdacent edges count as diagonals), so one for each edge that's not adjacent to the vertex, $n-2$.

What about with concave polygons? Is it the same rule? The proof is a proof by induction, but it's really hard to come up with.

> **Simple polygon**: a polygon with no holes and with no edges crossing over each other (the polygon is not self-crossing).

> **Theorem**: Every simple polygon $P$ with $n$ vertices can be triangulated in $n-2$ triangles.
> **Proof by induction**
> * Base: a polygon with $n=3$ vertices (a triangle);
> * Induction: if a polygon with $p<m$ vertices is triangulatable with $p-2$ triangles, then a polygon with $m$ vertices is triangulatable with $m-2$ triangles.
> We know that a triangle can be triangulated in $n-2=1$ triangle with $n-3=0$ diagonals (all possible vertex pairs already form edges).
> To prove the induction step, let's work backwards, starting from a large $m$.
> 1. Pick the vertex $V$ with smallest $x$ ($O(n)$);
> 2. Find its neighbors $u$ and $w$ ($O(1)$ if we have an ordered array of vertices);
> 3a. $uw$ is a diagonal ($O(n)$ to find out);
> 4a. I have split the polygon into a triangle and a polygon with $m-1$ vertices, triangulatable with $m-1-2$ triangles;
> 5a. The full polygon can be triangulated with those triangles, plus the other one we found in 4a, in total $m-1-2+1=m-2$ triangles, QED.
> 3b. $uw$ is not a diagonal ($O(n)$ to find out);
> 4b. There's at least one vertex inside the triangle $uvw$, find the one closest to $v$ and call it $v'$;
> 5b. Fantastic geometric trick: draw a line parallel to $uw$ passing through $v'$; $uv$, $vw$ and that line bind a triangle that contains no vertex, $vv'$ is a diagonal that splits the polygon into two smaller polygons with $m_1<m$ and $m_2<m$ vertices, triangulatable with, by assumption, $m_1-2$ and $m_2-2$ triangles, so the whole polygon can be split in $m_1+m_2-4$ triangles, but $m_1+m_2$ is $m+2$ because the two vertices $v$ and $v'$ are in common and thus are double counted, so $P$ can be triangulatted in $m+2-4=m-2$ triangles, QED.

The camera problem has been proved to be NP-hard, no polynomial solution, that means combinatorial explosion, intractable. So we can't find the *smallest*, we should aim for something *reasonably small*, that being the triangulation. So we can place a camera in each triangle. But actually, we can put a camera on each diagonal, each diagonal controls two triangles. But actually, even then there's overlap, because triangles can have multiple diagonals each. But if we put a camera on a vertex, it covers multiple diagonals at once. But there are more vertices than diagonals. But we don't **have** to put a camera on **every** vertex.
