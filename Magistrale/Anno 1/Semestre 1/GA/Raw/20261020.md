# Polygon triangulation

We've more or less solved the problem. We started from the gallery guarding problem, we realized that the best way to solve it is to triangulate the polygon, triangulate the vertices, and place cameras on just one color, giving us at most $\lceil \tfrac n 3 \rceil$ cameras.

We don't just want to solve the problem, we want to solve the problem in the best possible way. And Chezelle o come si chiama proved that the best way is $O(n+f(r))$, where $r$ is the number of reflex vertices and $f()$ is some function we don't care about. What happens when our polygon is convex? There are no reflex vertices, making the complexity $O(n)$. And we do indeed have a simple way to triangulate a convex polygon, by just picking one vertex and connecting it to all non-adjacent vertices, which is $n-2$, $O(n)$.

The method to triangulate convex polygons also works on polygons with holes, but we only care about simple polygons.

Except there are two left. The evil guys.

* ==🟡Split==: reflex angle with two smaller neighbors.
* ==🔴Merge==: reflex angle with two larger neighbors.

They're called split because if I draw a diagonal from an end vertex, and it needs to be an upwards diagonal, and it splits the polygon in two parts, but the end vertex is still an end vertex, I didn't gain anything. If I do that with a split vertex, the split vertex becomes a regular vertex.

You can do the same with downwards diagonals from merge vertices. Together they're called turn vertices.

How much does it cost to find the other end point of the diagonal to remove a turn vertex? $O(n)$. There are $O(n)$ turn vertices, the whole thing is $O(n^2)$. All of this to once again find an $O(n^2)$ algorithm? No please, we're here to improve the efficiency!

Objective: find an efficient strategy to identify the other end-point of the diagonals from turn vertices.

In cases like this, an easy way to reduce complexity is to order the set, because it turns finding an element from $O(n)$ to $O(\log n)$.

Our polygon is given to us as an ordered list of vertices based on their clockwise order, we can order it in a different way.

To order the vertices we use the ==sweeping line algorithm==: imagine a straight horizontal line starting above the polygon and going down, it first touches a single point forming a 0-length segment, then the length grows, and once it finds another vertex it's still a single segment, but it finds the split vertex and ohhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhh it splits into two segments, and when it finds a merge vertex two segments merge into one. When I get to an end vertex, a piece disappears, a segment ends, and when I get to a start vertex a new segment appears (starts).

I can easily draw a diagonal upwards when I find a split vertex, because I already know the previous vertices.

I want a diagonal, how did we define a diagonal? ... . So the candidate is the ==lowest== vertex ==over== the split vertex ==in between== the edges immediately to the left and the right of the split vertex.
